Every product I've designed had stakeholders. None of them were as demanding as Alma and Eleen.

When I built Siba List, a daily learning list tied to their allowance, I thought the hard part would be the interface. It wasn't. The hard part was that my users don't fill in feedback forms. They just stop using the thing.

## Checkmarks lie, proof doesn't

The first version was a list with checkboxes. Within a week, every box was ticked and nothing was done. Not because my daughters are dishonest, but because a checkbox asks for nothing.

So the app started asking for proof: a photo of the page, a recording of the piano piece, a timer that actually ran. Completion stopped being a claim and became a small, visible thing.

> If your product only asks people to say they did something, you're measuring politeness, not behaviour.

## Points beat money (at first)

Paying per task turned every task into a negotiation. Points made it a game. Then Alma asked for a better exchange rate, and I realised I'd designed an economy. Talks are ongoing.

## Give people a way out

The feature they love most is the one that lets them skip: one free pass a week. A rule with a release valve gets followed. A rule without one gets argued with every Friday.

## What I took back to work

- Ask for evidence of the behaviour, not a claim about it.
- Make progress visible before you make it valuable.
- Design the exception on purpose, or users will design it for you.

My clients at work are easier. They at least tell me when something is boring.
