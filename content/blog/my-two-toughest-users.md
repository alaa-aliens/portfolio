Every product I've designed had stakeholders. None of them were as demanding as Alma and Eleen.

They have a daily list: coding, a language, music (piano for Alma, guitar for Eleen) and a couple of pages of a book. Each item gets proved with a screenshot or a voice note sent to me, and the list is tied to their weekly allowance. When I designed [Siba List](/work/siba-list) to replace that system, I thought the hard part would be the interface. It wasn't.

## The rule everyone hated, including me

The old rule was all-or-nothing: finish everything, get the allowance; miss one thing, get nothing. Even I thought that was unfair. One bad Tuesday wiped out a good week, and once a week was lost, there was no reason to try for the rest of it.

## Points instead of checkmarks

So every task became worth points. A perfect week is 240. The week decides what share of the allowance she keeps, on a banded ladder, so a bad Tuesday costs a little instead of everything.

> A rule that punishes one mistake with total loss doesn't teach consistency. It teaches giving up on Wednesday.

## Proof belongs next to the task

Proofs used to arrive in a chat with everything else and get lost. Now she attaches the screenshot or the recording to the task itself, and I get a review queue. Anything I ignore for 48 hours approves itself, because a slow parent shouldn't cost a kid her points.

## Let the app watch the clock

"30 minutes of piano" used to mean me watching a clock. Now the timer runs in the app and the task only completes when it ends. Thirty minutes I got back, twice a week.

## Design the way out

One free pass a week, and it can be used after the fact, because that's when you actually need it. A rule with a release valve gets followed. A rule without one gets argued with every Friday.

## Everything is negotiable

Points can become money, play time, or something from a rewards menu. Cashing out is a request, which I approve. Alma immediately asked for a better exchange rate. Talks are ongoing.

## What I took back to work

- If one mistake costs everything, people stop trying. Make the cost proportional.
- Put the evidence next to the work it proves, not in a separate channel.
- Design the exception on purpose, or users will design it for you.

My clients at work are easier. At least they tell me when something's boring before they stop using it.
