At home, studying ran on a manual loop. The girls photographed their textbook pages. I turned the pages into a summary and a quiz, sent it over iMessage, then marked every answer by hand. Now repeat that for two kids, every subject, every week.

It worked, barely. Nothing was saved, nobody else could see progress, and every quiz cost me an evening. I wanted one place where each child studies, and where a parent stays in control of what goes in front of them.

That place became [Siba](/work/siba).

## Two questions before every screen

Siba exists because of two people, and every screen started as a question about one of them. Would Alma open this without being asked? Would Eleen get it before she can read every word on it?

Those two questions killed more of my ideas than any client ever has.

## A name with a story

Siba (سيبا) is named after Sibawayh, the grammarian of Arabic. I like the idea of a study app carrying the name of someone who spent his life explaining a language clearly.

## A study buddy, not a teacher

Siba the character is the girls' study buddy, never their teacher. She has a pose for every moment, so the app can react the way a friend would: coffee in the morning, thinking along during a lesson, a gentle look when an answer is wrong. Her voice says each girl's name. "Ready, Alma? Let's learn something new together!"

## More ways in than reading

Reading a summary is one way to learn, and for a lot of kids it's the least fun one. A lesson in Siba can be read, flipped as cards, matched, put in order, drawn as a mind map, listened to, or played as a quiz. Different subjects, different kids, different days.

## Parents publish, kids play

Either side can upload pages, but only a parent publishes a lesson. Everything starts as a draft a parent can check and edit. That one rule is what made me comfortable putting it in front of my own children.

## The first quiet evening

The best moment of the whole project wasn't a screen. It was the first night I didn't mark a single quiz. Relief.

> The toughest usability testers I've ever had don't care about UX at all. They just know when something's boring.
