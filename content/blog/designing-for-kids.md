Designing [Siba](/work/siba) for my daughters meant designing for two very different users at once: an older girl who reads everything, and a younger one who can't read every word yet. Eleen, the youngest tester, gets her own early-years mode. Here are the rules that came out of it.

## Read it to them

Younger kids get the summary read aloud. If the lesson only works for children who already read well, it's not a lesson for young children, it's a reading test.

## Tap, don't type

Challenges for younger kids are tap-tile puzzles instead of typed answers. Typing is a skill of its own, and a lesson about animals shouldn't fail because of spelling.

## Amber, not red

A wrong answer is amber, never red. Red means danger and failure to most of us, and it means it even more to a seven-year-old. Amber says "not quite" without the alarm.

## Never say "exam"

The word "exam" never appears in the young mode. Same quiz, different word. Kids already have enough exams; studying at home shouldn't feel like another one.

## Sound that rewards without startling

Sound was designed like the character: to reward without startling. The interface sounds are tiny, soft tones. A wrong answer gets a gentle two-note "hmm", never a buzzer. A buzzer teaches kids to be afraid of trying.

## A friend, not a judge

The character never scolds. She reacts like a friend would, with a gentle look when an answer is wrong and a cheer when it's right, and she says the child's name. Being seen is a better motivator than being graded.

## Colours that mean one thing

Chunky buttons with real depth, so they look pressable. And gold only ever means points. When a colour means one thing everywhere, kids learn the interface faster than any tutorial could teach it.

> None of these rules are only for kids. Adults also hate buzzers, red error screens and the word "exam". We're just better at pretending we don't mind.
